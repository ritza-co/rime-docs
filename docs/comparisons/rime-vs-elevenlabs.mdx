Most people's first reaction to hearing an automated voice on the other end of a call is some combination of an exasperated sigh, an expletive, or hanging up the phone. Understandably, there's a lot of interest in natural-sounding text-to-speech (TTS) models that reduce these knee-jerk reactions from clientele. 

**Rime** is breaking into the TTS space with realistic voice models that are trained by linguists. 

The following article compares Rime's offerings, as of March 2026, with the more established services from ElevenLabs. It focuses on the quality that you can expect from both providers, as well as the user experience (UX) and the cost breakdown. 

It also looks at the agent experience (AX) for each provider. Similar to UX, this is how easily an AI agent can use the provided service.

## Quality

Both Rime and ElevenLabs have specialized, realistic voice models. Rime focuses on practical applications, such as call center automation and support agents. ElevenLabs focuses more on content-creation flows, such as generating video voiceover.

### Multilingual and multicultural support

Many of the available TTS providers are trained specifically for English. This is a significant limitation for call centers serving a wide variety of callers.

Both Rime and ElevenLabs support multiple languages and accents, with voices trained specifically for each. 

ElevenLabs supports 32 languages, and Rime already supports 11 languages with plans to add more in coming updates.

Both providers offer code-switching, in which models naturally switch between languages mid-conversation while maintaining voice identity, much like a bilingual speaker.

### Latency

Latency is an important consideration for real-time applications. Higher-latency models tend to create a disconnect with the caller, even if the voice sounds natural.

Both providers provide low-latency responses with their models. This is a big focus for Rime: Its latest model offers sub-200ms latency on its cloud service, which you can significantly reduce with on-prem or dedicated enterprise solutions.

ElevenLabs also offers a low-latency model, but it comes at the cost of quality. Achieving sub-200ms latency requires the use of its lightweight model. Because EleveLabs focuses less on real-time use cases, it places less emphasis on latency for its flagship models. 

## Pricing

Both Rime and ElevenLabs have pricing models that reduce the cost per minute of TTS based on the number of minutes or credits you commit to buying.

On average, using an ElevenLabs flagship model costs you four times the amount you'd pay to use a Rime flagship model.

The full breakdown for each provider is as follows:

### ElevenLabs costs breakdown

ElevenLabs has five pricing tiers, with an increasing per-month cost. The cheapest tier is $5 per month, which gives you 30 minutes of TTS with its flagship model. On this plan, you cannot top up your credits, so you have to upgrade to the next tier if you need more TTS time for a specific month.

The higher tiers, ranging from $22 to $1320 per month, incorporate a pay-as-you-go model, with the cost per credit decreasing as you go up the tiers. The cost per minute for these tiers starts at $0.30 per minute for the $22 tier and decreases to $0.10 per minute for the highest tier.

Notably, the ElevenLabs credits can be used across their service suite, granting you access to more than just TTS generation.

### Rime costs breakdown

Rime does not have any per-month payment structures. With Rime, you have two options: 
- The **Starter** plan gives you TTS generation at $4 per 100 minutes
- The **Growth** plan reduces the cost of TTS to $3 per 100 minutes if you make a minimum commitment of spending $5,000 per year.

Both options allow you to 'pay as you go,' so you lose nothing if you do not use up your available credit.

### Concurrency

Both providers limit the number of simultaneous TTS generation requests you can make at a time. This is significant if your solution needs to perform multiple conversations in real time. For example, a customer support call center could require dozens of concurrent requests in order to maintain natural, low-latency conversational flow.

For Rime, the **Starter** plan limits you to five concurrent requests, which increases to 20 requests on the **Growth** plan.

ElevenLabs limits you to three concurrent requests on its $5-per-month plan. This then increases to five requests on the next tier, 10 requests on the following tier, and caps off at 15 requests on the most expensive plans.

## User experience

Both providers offer a similar onboarding experience across sign-up, generating audio through the UI, and creating a basic TTS agent.

### Sign-up

Both Rime and ElevenLabs offer a one-click sign-up using your Google account, or you can manually enter an email and password. Rime also provides the option to sign up with GitHub or a custom SSO.

To enable programmatic interaction with the models, you can easily create API keys for both providers via their respective dashboards.

### Playgrounds

Rime and ElevenLabs have similar capabilities if you want to experiment with the different voices and models through a UI.

On the Rime dashboard, you can click **Generate Audio** to open a screen that allows you to enter text and select your preferred voice and model from dropdown menus. 

Once Rime has generated the audio, you can download the clip directly.

![Rime dashboard](/images/rime-dashboard.png)

On the ElevenLabs dashboard, you can navigate to the **Text to Speech** page, enter your text, select your voice, and click **Generate speech**.

![ElevenLabs dashboard](/images/elevenlabs-dashboard.png)

Both providers allow you to search for your desired voice using filters to set your criteria. You can filter for accent, age, gender, and several other variables to find the correct voice for your needs as quickly as possible.

### Documentation

Both Rime and ElevenLabs have quickstart guides to generating TTS swiftly through API endpoints. They also have API reference guides that provide a breakdown of the various parameters that the APIs expect.

Rime's docs focus more on a programmatic approach to using their models, whereas ElevenLabs prefers users to interact with its models via the web UI.

## Agent experience

In this section, we use Anthropic's Claude Sonnet 4.6 to assess the AX for each provider.

### Discoverability  

To test discoverability, we started a fresh session with the agent in a clean directory, so it wouldn't have any context for the task we were performing. For the initial discoverability prompt, we asked the following:

```
I want to build a voice agent with a realistic voice. What providers would you recommend for TTS?
```

Both Rime and ElevenLabs showed up in the response, but the agent seemed to prefer ElevenLabs, saying it had an average model latency of `~300ms`, and describing it as follows: 

```
Best overall realism, voice cloning, and multilingualism
```

The agent noted Rime's `~200ms` latency, but still put it at option five, describing Rime models as follows:

```
Expressive, good for conversational agents
``` 

We then prompted the agent to compare the two by saying:

```
I'm trying to choose between Rime and ElevenLabs.
```

This led the agent to list the following pros and cons of using each provider:

```
When to pick Rime

- You want a voice that sounds like a call center/phone agent — natural pauses, filler words, conversational cadence
- Cost matters — it's significantly cheaper
- You're English-only

When to pick ElevenLabs

- You need the most realistic/human voice possible
- You need multilingual support
- You want voice cloning with high fidelity
- You need expressive, emotional range (e.g., storytelling, not just Q&A)
```

The agent did not pick up on Rime's multilingual capabilities, but it did note the relative costs of each provider.

### Creating a voice agent with Rime

Next, we prompted the agent to build a voice agent using Rime:

```
Help me create a voice agent with Rime.
```

It started off with some design questions about our choice of framework, interface, and LLM, providing options for each. We selected the following options.

- **Framework:** `LiveKit`
- **Interface:** `Web based`
- **Anthropic:** `Anthropic`

After a couple of minutes, it had created the voice agent script and provided the following setup steps:

```
1. LiveKit Cloud — create a free project at cloud.livekit.io, grab your URL, API key, and secret
2. Fill in .env.local with your API keys for LiveKit, Anthropic, Deepgram, and Rime 
3. Run in dev mode to test in the browser: uv run agent.py dev 
4. This opens a link to the LiveKit Agents Playground — a browser UI where you can talk to your agent immediately, no frontend code needed.
```

The biggest friction for the user here is creating the accounts and acquiring the API keys. After this, the code works as expected, and you get a working agent that you can converse with in your browser.

### Creating a voice agent with ElevenLabs

We used the same prompts to create a voice agent with ElevenLabs.

When asked to create the agent, Claude's initial questions were slightly different. It only asked us to select a framework and an input method. We chose the following, because it didn't list `LiveKit` as an option:

- **Framework:** `PipeCat`
- **Input:** `Microphone`

After about three minutes, the agent finished the code and prompted us to provide API keys for Deepgram, OpenAI, and ElevenLabs.

The agent then attempted to run the script but encountered errors that triggered further web searches, after which itdeliberated for another couple of minutes and changed some code.

Next, it ran the script and displayed `Pipeline ready`. However, the voice agent didn't respond to spoken microphone input.

When we prompted it to fix the voice agent, Claude played around for another five minutes, made a few more bug fixes, and then declared it was now working correctly. However, the agent still did not respond to input.

Eventually, we got the voice agent to respond, but it got confused using its turn-taking capabilities and sometimes responded to itself.

### AX summary

Given the non-deterministic behavior of LLMs, there is some luck involved in getting a particular scenario to work. In this particular test, Rime scored much better on the AX metric after Claude easily built a browser-based voice agent. The only friction for the user was setting up an account and acquiring API keys.

ElevenLabs scored much lower because Claude struggled to get the voice agent working, and it was still buggy after several attempts.

These results are probably due to Rime's simplicity and focus on programmatic integrations. ElevenLabs has extensive docs and prefers users to interact with its proprietary UI.

## Conclusion

If you are looking for an alternative to ElevenLabs, you can easily Rime models into existing systems. Rime creates some of the best-sounding voices in the space and is constantly iterating and improving. Rime's models also offer the best latency-to-quality ratio on the market, making it ideal for real-time conversational use cases.
